Nomenclature & Isomerism of Organic Compounds

Chemistry · Class 10 · Unit 1 — Complete Study Tool

Chapter Summary

  • The huge variety of organic compounds arises from carbon's ability to bond with itself (catenation) and with elements such as hydrogen, oxygen and nitrogen.
  • Compounds with the same molecular formula can have different structural formulas (different arrangement of branches).
  • In IUPAC nomenclature, the longest carbon chain is treated as the parent chain; the rest are treated as branches (alkyl groups).
  • Carbon atoms must be numbered so that the branches get the lowest possible locants.
  • If the same branch occurs more than once, use the prefixes di, tri, tetra, and separate the locants with commas.
  • A hyphen (–) must separate numbers from letters in an IUPAC name.
  • Chains with a double bond (C=C) are called alkenes (suffix –ene); chains with a triple bond (C≡C) are called alkynes (suffix –yne). The bond must get the lowest possible locant.
  • A functional group is the atom or group of atoms attached to carbon that determines a compound's characteristic chemical and physical properties.
  • The chain containing the functional group is treated as the parent chain, and the functional group must get the lowest possible locant.
  • Compounds with the same molecular formula but different properties are called isomers; this phenomenon is called isomerism.

Two Example Hydrocarbons

CH₃–CH₂–CH₂–CH₂–CH₃  (Hydrocarbon I — straight chain)

CH₃–CH(CH₃)–CH₂–CH₃  (Hydrocarbon II — branched)

Both have the same molecular formula C₅H₁₂, but different structural formulas — this is the basis of chain isomerism.

Naming Rules & Formulas

1. Branched Alkanes

Locant of branch + hyphen + name of alkyl group + root word + suffix (ane)
  • Step 1: Choose the longest chain containing the most carbon atoms as the parent chain.
  • Step 2: Number the chain (from left or right) so the branched carbon gets the lowest possible locant.
  • Step 3: If the same branch appears more than once, use di (2), tri (3), tetra (4) and separate locants with commas.
  • Step 4: Use a hyphen between a number and a letter.

2. Unsaturated Hydrocarbons

Alkene = root word + hyphen + locant of double bond + hyphen + ene
Alkyne = root word + hyphen + locant of triple bond + hyphen + yne

Numbering should be done so the bond gets the lowest possible locant.

  • e.g. CH₂=CH–CH₂–CH₃ → But-1-ene
  • e.g. CH₃–CH=CH–CH₃ → But-2-ene
  • e.g. CH≡C–CH₂–CH₃ → But-1-yne

Isomerism — Types

Definition: Compounds with the same molecular formula but different chemical and physical properties are called isomers, and the phenomenon is called isomerism. Their structural formulas are different.

1. Chain Isomerism

Compounds with the same molecular formula but a different arrangement (branching) of the carbon chain.

CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₃ (n-butane)
CH₃CH(CH₃)CH₃ (2-methylpropane)

2. Position Isomerism

Compounds with the same molecular formula and the same functional group, but the position of the functional group/bond differs.

CH₃CH₂CH₂OH (propan-1-ol)
CH₃CH(OH)CH₃ (propan-2-ol)

3. Functional Isomerism

Compounds with the same molecular formula but different functional groups.

CH₃CH₂OH (ethanol — alcohol)
CH₃–O–CH₃ (methoxymethane — ether)

4. Metamerism

Isomerism between compounds with the same molecular formula and the same functional group (–O– or >C=O), but with different alkyl groups on either side of that group.

CH₃CH₂–O–CH₂CH₃ (ethoxyethane)
CH₃–O–CH₂CH₂CH₃ (methoxypropane)
