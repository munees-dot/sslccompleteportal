Class X · English · Unit 2 — Poem

The Seedling

Paul Laurence Dunbar

Let's Read and Enjoy · Perseverance in the Face of Adversity

About the Poem

'The Seedling' is the poem in Unit 2, a unit about turning problems into opportunities and becoming solution providers who transform the world. The poem asks a simple question: can we stay motivated when the path to success is long, difficult and challenging?

Dunbar answers through the story of a tiny seedling lying in the dark soil. It admits it is not strong, yet decides to do its best. Step by step — a leaflet, a stem, more leaves — it grows into a plant and finally a flower. The poet then turns to children and asks them to be like the seedling: to work hard, share life's labour, and trust that help will come through the lonely, struggling hours.

About the Poet

Paul Laurence Dunbar (1872–1906) was an American poet, novelist and short story writer. He was enslaved in Kentucky before the American Civil War. He published his first poem, 'Our Martyred Soldiers', at the age of sixteen, and became one of the first African-American writers to win international acclaim.

He worked with the Wright brothers on their publication The Tatler. His first book of poems, Oak and Ivy, appeared in 1893. He went on to publish many collections of poems, short stories, four novels and a play.

The Poet's Message in One Line

Weakness is not a reason to stop — begin with "I'll do the best I can", keep growing, and the sun and showers of life will help you bloom.

Stanza-wise Explanation

Stanza 1 — A small, silent seedling lies in the dark soil (its "darksome bed"). It begins to talk to itself — the poet gives it a voice.
Stanza 2 — The seedling honestly admits it is not strong, but resolves to do its best. That single decision starts its "work of life" — its growth.
Stanza 3 — It pushes up a tiny leaflet into the daylight to look around and to lead the way for the other parts to follow. It acts like a brave scout.
Stanza 4 — The leaflet likes what it sees and calls its "brother", the Stem. Two more leaflets hear and quickly follow. One brave start inspires others.
Stanza 5 — Growing so fast is hard work — the seedling "sweats and pants". But before it realises it, it has become a plant. Effort brings results almost without our noticing.
Stanza 6 — Nature helps: sunshine pours down and clouds give rain. The plant keeps growing until it blossoms into a flower — the reward of steady effort.
Stanza 7 — The poet speaks directly to children: be like the seedling, always do your best. Children too must share the work of life, just as adults do.
Stanza 8 — Help (sun and showers) will come during lonely, difficult times, until you grow the lasting flowers of virtue — good character that never fades.

Summary (English)

A quiet little seedling lies in the dark earth and begins talking to itself. It admits that it is not very strong, but it promises to do the best it can. From that moment, its work of life begins.

It pushes a small leaflet up into the daylight to examine the surroundings and show the others the way. The leaflet likes what it sees and calls its brother, the Stem. Two more leaflets follow quickly. The hurry makes the seedling sweat and pant, but almost before it knows it, it has become a plant.

The sun shines on it and the clouds give it rain, and the little plant keeps growing until it becomes a flower. The poet then addresses children directly: be like the seedling and always do your best. Every child must share life's labour just as every adult does. The sun and showers will help them through lonely, difficult hours, until they bring out the beautiful, lasting flowers of virtue.

Theme: Self-motivation, perseverance and a positive attitude lead to success. Even the weak can achieve greatness if they begin with determination and keep trying; nature and life offer support to those who make the effort.

Tone: Optimistic, encouraging and instructive (didactic).

Poetic Devices (Activity 2)

Poetic DeviceExamples from the PoemExplanation
Imagery"pushed a little leaflet / Up into the light of day"; "The sunshine poured upon it"; "Made the seedling sweat and pant"Creates vivid images of optimistic expression — we can see the seedling's growth and feel its effort.
Alliteration"little leaflet", "haste and hurry", "sun and showers", "fair, unfading flowers"Repetition of the same consonant sound at the beginning of nearby words; adds music and rhythm.
Personification"To itself it fell a-talking"; "The leaflet liked the prospect"; "called its brother, Stem"; "sweat and pant"Human qualities are given to the seedling and its parts, making its struggle feel like our own.
Metaphor"darksome bed" (the soil); "Virtue's fair, unfading flowers"The soil is a bed; good qualities are flowers that never fade.
SymbolismSeedling = a child / a beginner; sun and showers = help, blessings and opportunities; flower = successObjects in nature stand for ideas about human life.
Apostrophe / Direct address"Little folks, be like the seedling"The poet speaks directly to children to deliver the moral.
Rhyme schemebed / said; can / began; day / way; Stem / them; pant / plant; shower / flower; can / man; hours / flowersabcb in every stanza — only the 2nd and 4th lines rhyme.

Metre & rhythm: The lines have a steady, song-like trochaic beat (stressed–unstressed: "As a quiet little seedling"). Long lines alternate with shorter indented lines, giving the poem the movement of a nursery song.

Archaic words: "a-talking", "darksome", "folks" give the poem an old-fashioned, story-telling charm.

Tone: Optimistic, gentle, encouraging and didactic.

Let's Rewind and Rejoice — Q & A

1. What triggered the seedling's 'work of life'?
Its decision and self-talk — "I am not so very robust, but I'll do the best I can." The moment it resolved to try its best in spite of its weakness, its work of life began.
2. What is inspiring about the seedling's attitude to life?
It honestly accepts that it is weak, yet it does not give up. It is positive, self-motivated and determined to do its best. It even wants to "show the rest the way", which makes it a leader.
3. Why was the seedling in a hurry to come out of the earth?
It was eager to see the world above, examine its surroundings and lead the way for the others. Once the leaflet liked the prospect, the whole seedling was keen to grow into the light.
4. How is the growth of the plant described in the poem?
Step by step: first a leaflet pushes into the daylight, then it calls its brother Stem, then two more leaflets follow. Working hard, it sweats and pants, and soon finds itself a plant. With the help of sunshine and rain, it keeps growing until it becomes a flower.
5. What message does the seedling give us?
Always do the best you can, however weak you may feel. Share life's labour, persevere through lonely and difficult times, and help will come; in the end you will achieve success and develop lasting virtue.

Let's Appreciate — Activity 1 (Discuss)

1. How does the poem relate to the challenges of life?
Like the seedling in the dark soil, everyone begins life with limitations and difficulties. The seedling's struggle to push through the earth mirrors our struggle against problems. Its success shows that challenges can be overcome with steady effort, and that support (sun and showers) comes to those who keep trying.
2. What does the poem tell us about the importance of self-motivation to achieve success?
Nobody pushes the seedling — it motivates itself with the words "I'll do the best I can." Success begins with that inner decision. Self-motivation keeps us going through "lonesome, struggling hours" when no one else is watching, and it leads to growth and achievement.

Activity 3 — Appreciation (Model)

Title: The Seedling

Introduction: 'The Seedling' is a short, inspiring poem by the American poet Paul Laurence Dunbar. Through the growth of a tiny seedling, the poet teaches children the value of effort, self-belief and perseverance.

Theme: A weak little seedling lying in the dark earth decides to do its best. It pushes up a leaflet, then a stem and more leaves, works hard, and becomes a plant. Helped by sunshine and rain, it finally blooms into a flower. The poet asks children to be like the seedling, to share life's labour, and to trust that help will come through difficult times until they bring out the unfading flowers of virtue. The theme is that self-motivation and perseverance lead to success.

Poetic devices: The poem uses personification — the seedling talks, the leaflet likes the prospect and calls its brother Stem, and the seedling sweats and pants. Alliteration adds music: "little leaflet", "haste and hurry", "sun and showers", "fair, unfading flowers". Vivid imagery lets us see the leaflet rising "into the light of day" and the sunshine pouring upon it. "Virtue's fair, unfading flowers" is a beautiful metaphor. The rhyme scheme is abcb, and the steady trochaic rhythm makes it sound like a song. The tone is optimistic and encouraging.

Conclusion: The poem is highly relevant today, when young people face competition and pressure. The lines I like most are "I am not so very robust, / But I'll do the best I can" because they show that success does not depend on strength but on the will to try. The poem leaves us hopeful and motivated.

Activity 4 — Paragraph: Importance of Self-motivation

Self-motivation is the inner drive that pushes us to act without waiting for others to push us. The seedling in Dunbar's poem is weak, yet it motivates itself and grows into a flower. Social entrepreneurs like Srikanth Bolla, who was born blind, and Arunachalam Muruganantham, who was mocked for his experiments, succeeded because they refused to give up. They turned their challenges into opportunities that changed thousands of lives. Self-motivation helps us stay focused when the path is long, bounce back from failure, and keep learning. Success rarely comes easily, but those who believe in themselves and keep trying will surely reach their goal.

Let's Listen — 'It Couldn't Be Done' vs 'The Seedling'

Both poems celebrate determination. In Edgar A. Guest's poem, a man is told a task is impossible, but he starts it cheerfully and completes it. Dunbar's seedling, though not robust, decides to do its best and grows into a flower. Both teach that a positive attitude and effort can overcome doubt and weakness — just as Srikanth Bolla overcame blindness and Muruganantham overcame ridicule to succeed.

  Up into the light of day,
To examine the surroundings
  And show the rest the way.
1. What does the seedling admit about itself?
It admits that it is not very robust (strong).
2. When did the seedling begin its "work of life"?
From the moment it decided to do the best it could.
3. Why did the seedling push a leaflet into the light of day?
A. To escape from the rain
B. To examine the surroundings and show the rest the way
C. To call the sun
D. To hide from the other plants
Correct answer: B

can – began; day – way
5. Identify the alliteration in the lines.
"little leaflet" (repetition of the 'l' sound)
Poem Comprehension4×1=4
Little folks, be like the seedling,
  Always do the best you can;
Every child must share life's labour
  Just as well as every man.

And the sun and showers will help you
  Through the lonesome, struggling hours,
Till you raise to light and beauty
  Virtue's fair, unfading flowers.
6. Who are the "little folks"?
Children.
7. What do "sun and showers" stand for?
Help, support, blessings and opportunities that come in life.
8. What does the poet mean by "Virtue's fair, unfading flowers"?
Good qualities and character, beautiful and lasting like flowers that never fade — the true reward of effort.
9. Name the poetic device in "Little folks, be like the seedling".
Simile (be like the seedling) / direct address to the reader.
